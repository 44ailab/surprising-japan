SURPRISING JAPAN

Fukubukuro: Japan's Lucky Bags

My first fukubukuro, I bought for the wrong reason: everyone else in the queue had one, and the queue was moving fast. It was the morning of January 2, the department store had just opened, and people around me were walking away hugging fat paper bags like lottery winners. I paid my money, took my bag, and opened it at home with the seriousness of a man defusing something. Inside: a perfectly good sweater in a colour I would never choose, a scarf I still own, and one item I could not identify for a week. That, I learned, is not a failed lucky bag. That is a completely normal one.

When

Fukubukuro go on sale in the first days of the New Year, when shops hold their first sales of the year — many from January 1, department stores traditionally from January 2. The popular ones do not last the morning. For famous brands and big electronics stores, people queue before opening, sometimes from before dawn, and the bags are gone within the hour.

What happens

A fukubukuro — a "lucky bag" — is a sealed bag or box of goods sold for a fixed price, with the promise that what is inside is worth more than you paid. The catch is the seal: traditionally, you cannot see the contents, the sizes may or may not suit you, and there are no exchanges for disappointment. Shops clear their shelves and last season's stock; buyers gamble on a bargain. Both sides understand the arrangement, which is why the gamble is taken cheerfully, as a New Year fortune in shopping form.

Not every bag is blind anymore. Many shops now sell visible fukubukuro, with the contents displayed or listed — the bargain without the roulette — and fashion brands often let you pick a size and a colour family. Electronics and variety stores still lean into the mystery. And every year produces the joke counterpart: the fukou-bukuro, the "unlucky bag," a pun on the name for a bag whose contents were so unwanted that the luck plainly went elsewhere. Posting an unlucky bag's contents has become its own small New Year tradition.

The queues have partly moved online. Stores now sell fukubukuro through their apps and websites, some by lottery, some gone in minutes, and the opening ritual has spread to resale apps, where unopened bags — and, more often, the individual surprises pulled from them — change hands through January. The gamble no longer has to end at your own front door.

Good to know

The honest way to read a fukubukuro is that you are buying the shop's judgement at a discount: the total is genuinely worth more than the price, but worth and wanted are different words, and the bag knows nothing about your wardrobe. Regulars buy from shops whose taste they already trust, which is less a gamble than a subscription with suspense. What I did not expect, my first year, was how much of the pleasure is in the opening itself — the family gathered round, the items held up one by one for judgement, the trading that starts immediately. The bag is an excuse for a ceremony. Mine ended, as most do, with one keeper, one gift for someone else, and one mystery item whose purpose the internet eventually explained.
