Shichi-Go-San: Japan's Festival for Children Aged 7, 5, and 3

On and around November 15, Japan's shrines fill with small children in beautiful formal clothes. Shichi-Go-San means "seven, five, three," and it celebrates children who reach those ages — in old times, when many children died very young, reaching them was a true reason to give thanks.

When

The day is November 15, but most families celebrate on a weekend near it, not on the day itself. Popular shrines and photo studios are fully booked weeks before.

What happens

Girls celebrate at three and at seven. Boys celebrate at five, and in many families also at three. A three-year-old wears a special padded vest over her kimono. A five-year-old boy wears hakama, formal wide trousers, and a jacket with the family crest. A seven-year-old girl wears a full kimono with a wide, adult-style belt called an obi, for the first time.

At the shrine, the family prays for the child's healthy growth. After that, each child carries a long paper bag with pictures of a crane and a turtle — both animals are symbols of long life. Inside the bag is chitose-ame, "thousand-year candy": long, thin red and white sticks of candy. The candy is long because the parents wish for a long life for the child.

The day has a rhythm. The clothes are rented and fitted, specialists dress the child and arrange the hair, and then the family goes to the shrine, where the child — suddenly looking very grown-up — prays with the family. Afterwards comes the photograph, and the candy in its long bag. For the child, it may be the first time they are the formal center of the whole family's attention, dressed like a small adult and treated with unusual seriousness. For the grandparents watching, that is exactly the point: the child who was once a fragile baby is standing at the shrine gate, seven, five, or three years old, and thriving.

Good to know

The professional photograph is a serious event. Families rent the clothes, and specialists dress the child and arrange the hair. Seven-year-old girls may also have makeup, and many families spend more money on the photographs than on anything else that day. Grandparents are important guests — the classic photograph is three generations together in front of the shrine gate.

In some families, the ages follow the old Japanese way of counting age, in which a baby is one year old at birth. So a child may celebrate a little earlier than you expect.
