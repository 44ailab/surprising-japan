Tsuyu: Japan's Rainy Season and the Hydrangea Weeks

Before the real summer heat arrives, Japan gets a fifth season of its own: tsuyu, the rainy season. For roughly a month, a front of warm, wet air sits over the islands, the sky stays grey, and the country learns to live with rain as a daily fact — and finds one flower that loves it.

When

The rain front moves slowly north. It reaches Okinawa in May, settles over most of the main islands through June, and usually lifts by the middle or end of July. The weather agency formally announces both the start and the end of tsuyu for each region, and the end — tsuyu-ake — is treated as news, because it means true summer has begun. Hokkaido, the northern island, largely escapes the season altogether.

What happens

Daily life adjusts. Umbrellas live by every door, laundry is hung indoors or taken to laundromats with dryers, and everyone wages a small war against mould — shoes, closets, and bathrooms all need watching in the humidity. Children hang teru teru bozu, small white cloth dolls, at the window to wish for sunshine tomorrow. And out in the countryside, the rain is put to work: this is the season when rice seedlings are planted in flooded fields, and the tsuyu rains fill the water the rice will need.

The season has one beloved compensation: the hydrangeas. Ajisai bloom exactly when everything else is grey, in great clouds of blue, purple, and pink along temple paths and garden walls. Temples famous for their hydrangeas fill with visitors carrying umbrellas and cameras — the flowers are considered at their best wet, with the rain still on them.

Tsuyu is not a festival and nobody celebrates it exactly, but it shapes the year as much as any holiday: it is the pause between spring and summer, the reason June weddings compete with rain plans, and the water supply the whole farming year depends on.

Good to know

The word tsuyu is written with the characters for "plum" and "rain" — it is the rain that falls while the plums are ripening. The hydrangeas, meanwhile, are natural soil scientists: their flowers bloom blue in acidic soil and pink in alkaline soil, so the same bush can change colour depending on where it is planted.

If you visit Japan in June, the honest advice is to plan like a local: flexible days, indoor options ready, waterproof shoes — and at least one rainy afternoon set aside for a hydrangea temple, which is a sight the sunny seasons simply cannot offer.
