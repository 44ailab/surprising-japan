Osechi: Japan's New Year Food Boxes

The first osechi box I ever opened, I made the beginner's mistake: I looked for something warm. Everything in the stacked lacquer boxes was cold, much of it was sweet, and some of it — a glistening pile of tiny fish, a solid slab of golden mash — I could not identify at all. I ate politely and understood nothing. Only later did someone explain what I had actually been given: not a meal, exactly, but a box of wishes, each dish standing for something a family hopes the new year will bring, and all of it engineered to survive three days without anyone going near the stove.

When

Osechi is eaten over the first days of January, the sanganichi — the first three days of the New Year. The cooking happens in the last rush of December, and then, deliberately, it stops. The boxes are opened on January 1 and picked at for days, the family returning to them between shrine visits and New Year callers.

What happens

The food comes in jubako, heavy lacquered boxes that stack in tiers — the stacking itself is part of the point, a wish for good fortune piled up, layer on layer. Inside, the dishes are packed tight in a bright grid, and almost every one carries a meaning, most of them riding on a pun.

Kuromame, glossy black soybeans simmered sweet, come first in many boxes: mame is an old word for health and sturdy diligence, so the beans wish for a year of working well and staying well. Kazunoko, crunchy herring roe, is a mass of tiny eggs, and stands for many children and a flourishing family line. Tazukuri, small sardines glazed crisp and sweet, mean a bountiful harvest — sardines were once spread on rice fields as fertiliser, and the name literally points at making paddies. Datemaki, a sweet rolled omelette with a burnished top, resembles a rolled scroll, so it wishes for learning and culture. And kurikinton, chestnuts in a golden mash of sweet potato, is the colour of treasure: its name can be read as "gold dumplings," a wish for wealth in the year ahead.

What ties the box together is not the wishes, though — it is the keeping. Osechi is seasoned to survive: simmered in sweet soy, vinegared, candied, dried down, almost nothing in it is delicate or raw. That is the design. Tradition held that the first three days of the year were no time for kitchen fires, and the person who cooked all year — in most homes, the mother — was finally, officially, off duty. The boxes fed everyone while she rested. Shops, for the same reason, stayed shut, and a family that had not stocked up went hungry in style or not at all.

Good to know

Almost nobody makes a full osechi from scratch anymore. Department stores and famous restaurants sell ready-made boxes that families order weeks ahead, some costing more than a good hotel night, and convenience stores take reservations for modest ones. Making a few favourite dishes at home and buying the rest is the common modern compromise. What surprised me, eating from a box across three days, was how the design holds up: the flavours do not fade, they settle, and the third day's kuromame tastes, if anything, better than the first. The box was never really about showing off. It was about buying three mornings of rest, paid for in advance, in December, with sugar and soy.
