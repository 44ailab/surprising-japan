SURPRISING JAPAN

Seasonal Food in Japan: What to Eat in Every Season

I once went back to a small Tokyo restaurant three months after my first visit, ordered "the same fish as last time", and was gently told that the fish was gone — not sold out, gone, finished, until next year. The menu had not been cut; it had been replaced, course by course, by a completely different one. That was my first real lesson in seasonal food in Japan: the menu belongs to the calendar. Almost every ingredient has its shun — the brief window, sometimes only a few weeks, when it tastes best — and kitchens, supermarket shelves, and home cooking all turn over as the shun moves on.

When

Shun is short by design. A food is at its peak for a matter of weeks, and the first of it to appear — the hatsumono, the season's first catch or first harvest — is prized above all. An old saying claims that eating the hatsumono adds seventy-five days to your life, and whether or not anyone believes it literally, the first strawberries, the first bonito, the first matsutake of the year still make the news. Restaurants rewrite their menus with the seasons, convenience stores rotate their shelves, and a dish that was everywhere in June may be impossible to find in August.

What happens

Spring tastes green and sweet. Bamboo shoots, takenoko, are dug in the morning and rushed to the pot before they toughen, and simmered with rice or wakame seaweed. Strawberries reach their peak, piled into cakes and parfaits. It is also the season of cherry-blossom sweets — pink mochi wrapped in a salted cherry leaf — eaten while the blossoms are out and gone the moment they fall.

Summer is built around beating the heat. The emblematic dish is unagi, grilled eel lacquered with sweet sauce, eaten as fuel against the exhaustion of the humid weeks. Around it: ayu, sweetfish grilled whole with salt; edamame boiled in the pod and eaten with cold drinks; chilled noodles; and watermelon, split at the beach in the blindfolded game of suika-wari.

Autumn is the season Japanese people call the appetite season, and it earns the name. Matsutake mushrooms — pine-scented, fiercely expensive, and impossible to farm — are grilled or steamed in rice while they last; the first time I was served one, the whole table went quiet for the first bite, the way people do for something that will not come again for a year. Pacific saury, sanma, comes back fat from northern waters and is grilled whole and eaten with grated radish. Add roasted sweet potatoes sold from trucks that sing through evening streets, chestnuts in rice, and persimmons ripening on bare branches in every garden.

Winter gathers people around a pot. Nabe hot pots — broth bubbling in the middle of the table, everyone cooking their own vegetables, tofu, and meat — are the default dinner of the cold months. Crab comes into season on the Sea of Japan side, where the opening of the snow crab fishery in November is a local event. Oysters fatten in the cold water, citrus ripens, and mikan mandarins appear by the box, eaten one after another at the kotatsu, the heated table nobody wants to leave.

Good to know

You do not need a fancy restaurant to eat seasonally in Japan — you need a supermarket. The produce section is arranged by shun, the fish counter changes with the catch, and even convenience-store rice balls and desserts rotate monthly. If a menu says shun or "seasonal special", that is the dish the kitchen is proudest of this month. And if you visit the same restaurant in a different season and the menu has changed completely, nothing has gone wrong: that is the system working exactly as intended.
