SURPRISING JAPAN

Japanese New Year Traditions: Hatsumode, Osechi, and Otoshidama

New Year, called Shogatsu, is the most important holiday in Japan. It is not one day but a whole season of decorations, special food, greeting cards, and family visits — and almost every part of it carries a small rule that nobody explains to visitors.

When

Most companies close from about December 29 to January 3. Families gather at home, or travel back to their parents' houses. The decorations go up before the year changes, and the first three days of January are the heart of the holiday.

What happens

Before the year changes, homes are decorated. Kadomatsu, arrangements of pine branches and bamboo, stand in pairs at the entrance to welcome the gods of the new year. A rope of rice straw, shimenawa, hangs above the door. Inside, kagami mochi — two round rice cakes stacked with a small bitter orange on top — sits on a stand as an offering.

For the first three days, families eat osechi: beautiful foods in stacked boxes, made or ordered before the holiday so nobody has to cook. Each food has a meaning. Black beans are for health, herring eggs are for many children, and the rolled omelet looks like old scrolls, so it stands for learning.

On the morning of January 1, the mailbox is full of nengajo, New Year's greeting cards, which the post office collects through December and delivers all on this one morning. Children receive otoshidama, money from parents and relatives in small decorated envelopes. Then comes hatsumode, the first shrine visit of the year, where people pray, draw a paper fortune called omikuji, and buy new protective charms.

Good to know

Kadomatsu are usually put up by December 28. December 29 is avoided because the word for "nine" sounds like the word for suffering, and December 31 is avoided too — a decoration put up that late is called a "one-night decoration," and people say it is rude to the gods. In most regions the decorations come down on January 7 in the east of Japan (Kanto) and January 15 in the west (Kansai).

Otoshidama has an unwritten price scale: roughly 500 to 1,000 yen for small children, up to 5,000 to 10,000 yen for high school students. And if you draw a bad fortune at the shrine, you tie the paper to a rack or tree branch there and leave the bad luck behind.
