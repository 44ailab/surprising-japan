Omikuji: Japan's Fortune Slips, From Great Blessing to Great Curse

The slip of paper costs about as much as a gumball, and for a few seconds it holds your whole year. Shake the box, draw a numbered stick, and a drawer slides open with your fortune inside: a ranking in big characters, and underneath it, small print about your health, your studies, your lost umbrella, and the person you are waiting for. This is omikuji, the paper fortune of Japan's shrines and temples — and the first thing to know is that the ranking is not the point. The advice underneath is.

When

You can draw an omikuji at most shrines and temples all year, but the great season is hatsumode, the first shrine visit of the year, when millions of people draw one in the first days of January. The busiest shrines set out extra boxes and extra racks, and the queue for a fortune can be longer than the queue to pray.

What happens

You drop in a small coin — usually 100 or 200 yen — shake a hexagonal box until a thin numbered stick slips out of the hole, and hand the number over, or find the drawer with your number on it. The slip inside carries a poem at many temples, a grade at the top, and then a row of short verdicts on the parts of a life: wishes, health, study, travel, business, love, lost things. The grades run from dai-kichi, great blessing, down through middle and small blessings to kyo, bad luck — and at some shrines, dai-kyo, great bad luck, waits at the very bottom.

There is no national ladder. The order of the grades varies from shrine to shrine — some rank plain kichi above chu-kichi, others the reverse — and the shrine association itself says the small print matters more than the grade. A good fortune goes home in your wallet, where the blessing is said to travel with you. A bad one gets folded into a thin strip and tied to the rack or pine branch provided, and left behind: the bad luck stays at the shrine, and you walk away without it.

Good to know

Some famous places are known for dealing out hard luck. Senso-ji in Asakusa, Tokyo's oldest temple, has a reputation for bad fortunes turning up far more often than at most shrines — regulars treat a good draw there as the real thing. If you draw a bad one anywhere, nobody expects you to be crushed: read the advice, which is usually sensible, tie the slip up neatly, and go and get something warm to eat. That, the shrine would tell you, is exactly what the system is for.
