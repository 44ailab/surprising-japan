Sapporo Snow Festival: February's Week of Giant Snow Sculptures

For one week every February, a city in Japan's snowy north builds a second city out of the snow itself: full-size castles, concert stages, and characters several storeys tall, carved in white, lit at night, and gone when the week ends. This is the Sapporo Snow Festival, and it began with almost nothing — in 1950, local school students built six snow statues in a park, in a city looking for a reason to enjoy its own winter.

When

The festival runs for about a week in early February. The most recent edition, the 76th, was held from February 4 to 11, 2026; the next year's dates are announced in advance each autumn, so check the official site before booking. February in Sapporo is deep winter — well below freezing day and night — and the cold is part of the machinery: it is what lets a building made of snow stand up for a week.

What happens

The main site is Odori Park, a green strip a kilometre and a half long through the centre of the city, filled end to end with snow and ice sculptures: the giant showpieces — some around fifteen metres tall — alongside rows of smaller works, including an international snow-sculpting contest and statues made by local citizens. At night the big sculptures are lit and used as screens for projection shows. A second site in the Susukino entertainment district specialises in ice carving, lit against the neon, and a third, the Tsudome site, is given over to playing in the snow itself, with slides and rafts.

About two million visitors come across the week, and walking the sites costs nothing. The sculptures are at their sharpest in the first days, before sun and crowds soften the edges — and then, when the week closes, they are knocked down, and the park goes back to being a park until the snow returns.

Good to know

See Odori Park twice: once in daylight, when the carving reads clearly, and once after dark, when the lights and projections turn the same sculptures into something else. Book rooms and flights months ahead — festival week is the city's fullest of the year — and dress for standing outdoors at minus ten: the sculptures are not the only things that freeze. February's festival is the far end of the season this book has followed since the first sunrise of January — winter, in Japan, goes out in style.
