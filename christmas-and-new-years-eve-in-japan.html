SURPRISING JAPAN

Christmas and New Year's Eve in Japan

December in Japan has two famous days that look familiar to foreign visitors, but are not the same. Christmas belongs to couples, fried chicken, and cake. The deep, family holiday feeling that visitors expect at Christmas is waiting a few days later, at the end of the year.

When

Christmas Eve, December 24, is the big day — and December 25 is a normal working day. The real holiday starts at the end of the year, with the great cleaning and New Year's Eve, Omisoka, on December 31.

What happens

In Japan, Christmas is not a religious family holiday. It is a bright commercial event, mainly for couples and friends. Restaurants are fully booked, streets shine with lights, and the classic Christmas dinner is not turkey — it is fried chicken. Kentucky Fried Chicken sells special Christmas sets, and people reserve them, often many weeks before, because the shops are so busy. Reservations start in November. After the chicken comes the Christmas cake: a white sponge cake with cream and strawberries, sold by every bakery and convenience store in the country.

Then the year turns serious. Homes do osoji, the great year-end cleaning — a deep cleaning of the whole house, so the new year can begin in a clean home. On December 31, families eat toshikoshi soba, long buckwheat noodles: the long noodles are a wish for a long life, and because soba breaks easily, people say the noodles cut off the hardships of the old year. Many families watch Kohaku Uta Gassen, the famous red-and-white singing contest, and at midnight temple bells ring 108 times — joya no kane. People say there are 108 human desires and troubles, and each sound of the bell removes one of them.

Good to know

If you want fried chicken on Christmas Eve and have no reservation, be ready to stand in a long line. This is completely serious. And everyone knows the other secret: Christmas cakes are sold at a big discount on December 26, and nobody is ashamed to buy one.

Osoji has its own common rules: clean from high places down to low places, finish before New Year's Eve, and clean the entrance and the water areas — the kitchen, the bath, and the toilet — with special care. At many temples, the 108 bell strikes are shared: 107 strikes before midnight, and the last strike in the new year. Some temples even let visitors pull the rope and ring the bell themselves.
