Setsubun: Japan's Bean-Throwing Festival

Setsubun is the day Japan pushes bad luck out of the house before spring. It involves a demon mask, a shower of roasted soybeans, and one very serious rule about a sushi roll: do not cut it, and do not say a word until it is finished.

When

Setsubun usually falls on February 3. On the old Japanese calendar, this is the last day of winter, the day before spring begins. Its job is to clear out the bad luck of the old season so the new one can start clean.

What happens

In homes with children, the evening event is mamemaki, bean throwing. One parent — often the father — wears a demon mask and plays the oni, the demon. The children shout "Oni wa soto! Fuku wa uchi!" ("Demons out! Good luck in!") and throw roasted soybeans at him, and also out of the front door. Afterwards, everyone eats some of the beans: the tradition says one bean for each year of your age, and some people eat one extra for the coming year.

The second star of the day is ehomaki, one thick sushi roll that is never cut into pieces. You hold the whole roll, face the lucky direction of this year — the eho, which changes every year — and eat it in silence, without stopping. From late January, supermarkets and convenience stores sell ehomaki in large piles.

The whole evening runs on a simple logic. The demon is driven out, the good luck is called in, the beans are eaten for the years you have lived, and the roll is eaten facing the direction where this year's luck lives. Nothing about it is quiet or tidy — beans end up in every corner of the house — but by the end of the night, the home has been loudly, cheerfully cleaned of its bad luck, and spring can begin.

Good to know

The beans are roasted soybeans, thrown inside the rooms and outside the house — so cleaning up afterwards is real work. For this reason, some families throw beans in small packets, or peanuts still in their shells. And the silence rule is harder than it sounds: in many families, someone starts laughing in the middle of the ehomaki, the silence breaks, and that becomes part of the fun.

At some shrines and temples, famous people and sumo wrestlers throw beans to big crowds. In some regions, people also hang a sardine head and a holly branch at the door, because demons are said to hate the smell of the fish and the sharp leaves.
