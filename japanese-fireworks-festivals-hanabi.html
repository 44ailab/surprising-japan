Japanese Fireworks Festivals: Hanabi, the Sound of Summer

Ask a Japanese person what summer sounds like, and many will say fireworks. All through July and August, towns and cities hold hanabi taikai — fireworks displays that are not a ten-minute finale, but a full evening: an hour or more of the sky catching fire, watched by whole neighbourhoods sitting on picnic sheets.

When

Most displays are held in July and August, and there are hundreds of them across the country, from small local shows to enormous ones. Tokyo's Sumida River Fireworks Festival is one of the most famous: it traces its history back to 1733, launches around 20,000 fireworks, and draws close to a million spectators along the river.

What happens

People arrive hours early and spread picnic sheets on riverbanks and in parks to claim their spot. Many wear yukata, light summer kimono, and buy dinner from the yatai food stalls that line the streets. When a particularly beautiful shell opens, the crowd shouts "Tamaya!" — the name of a famous fireworks maker from the Edo period. A rival house, Kagiya, gets its own shout too. The makers' names have outlived the makers by centuries.

The show itself is choreographed in acts, with pauses between them, new designs shown off each year, and shells shaped to burst into hearts, smiling faces, and even cartoon characters when they open.

Fireworks in Japan began as something close to a prayer — the early river displays were held to comfort the spirits of the dead and to ward off plague and famine. The mood today is pure festival, but the scale still feels ceremonial: this is a whole town, looking up at the same sky at the same time.

Good to know

Fireworks here are also a craft, judged like one. At competitions such as the one in Omagari, Akita, fireworks makers from across Japan compete for national titles with their newest shells. And in Katakai, Niigata, they fire the yonshakudama, one of the largest fireworks shells in the world, which opens about 800 metres wide.

Practical warnings: strong wind can cancel a show at the last minute, the best viewing spots fill up by mid-afternoon, and the trains home afterwards are among the most crowded of the year. Japanese summer evenings are planned around all three facts.
