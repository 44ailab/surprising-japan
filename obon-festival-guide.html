Obon: Japan's Festival of the Dead

In the middle of August, the dead come home. Obon is the season when the spirits of family ancestors return to this world, and the whole country slows down to receive them — with small fires, vegetable animals, and dancing in the summer night.

When

Most of the country holds Obon in mid-August, around August 13 to 16. Obon is not an official national holiday, but most companies close for several days, and millions of people travel back to their family homes in the great summer trip called kisei. Trains and highways are extremely busy. Tokyo and some other areas hold Obon in July, following the old calendar.

What happens

At home, the family prepares a small altar. In most regions, on August 13, the family lights a small welcome fire, mukaebi, at the gate or the front door, to guide the spirits into the house. On the altar stand two small animals made of vegetables: a horse made from a cucumber, and a cow made from an eggplant, both with thin wooden legs. The horse is fast, so the ancestors can come home quickly. The cow is slow, so they can go back slowly and gently. On August 16, the family lights a send-off fire, okuribi, to show the spirits the way back. In Kyoto, huge fires in the shape of characters are lit on five mountains on that night.

For the days in between, the altar is the center of the house. The ancestors are guests: welcomed with fire at the door, given their fast horse and their slow cow, and finally seen off with fire again. The whole visit is framed by those two small flames.

In the evenings, neighborhoods gather in school grounds and temple gardens for bon odori, Bon dancing. People dance in big circles around a tower with lanterns and a drum on top. The steps are simple, and the same movements repeat again and again.

Good to know

You do not need to know the dance before you join. Stand in the circle, watch the person in front of you, and copy the movements. Local people are happy when a visitor joins the circle.

If you travel in Obon week, reserve early — the whole country is moving at the same time, back to family homes. And notice how the festival holds two feelings at once: it is a family reunion with the dead, and also the liveliest dancing week of the summer.
