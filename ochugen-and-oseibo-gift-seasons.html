Ochugen and Oseibo: Japan's Gift-Giving Seasons

Twice a year, gratitude in Japan becomes a season with its own shop floors. In summer it is called ochugen, in December oseibo — and both times, millions of beautifully wrapped boxes of beer, ham, coffee, and juice travel across the country carrying a single message: thank you for looking after me.

When

Ochugen, the mid-year gift, is sent from early July into mid-August — the exact weeks differ by region, earlier in the east and around the Obon holidays of August in much of the west. Oseibo, the year-end gift, goes out in the first half of December, timed to arrive before the New Year. The two seasons bookend the working year: one thanks people at its midpoint, the other closes the year with thanks.

What happens

The gifts go to the people you are indebted to — your boss, your teacher, your clients, the relatives who help your family — and they are almost always things that get used up: sets of beer or juice, ham and sausages, coffee, cooking oil, detergent, fine sweets, or fruit. Department stores open enormous gift centres for each season, with catalogues, tasting corners, and staff whose only job is wrapping and addressing. The box is delivered with formal noshi paper on top, printed with the season's name.

Choosing is its own quiet skill. The gift must be good, but not so lavish it becomes a burden; useful, because a gift that gathers dust helps nobody; and appropriate to the relationship — the standard catalogue sets exist precisely so that thousands of people can say the same thank you at the same safe, thoughtful level.

Underneath the formality, the custom is simple bookkeeping of the heart. Twice a year, you stop and count the people who carried you through the last six months — and you send the count out in boxes.

Good to know

These gifts are thanks, not exchanges: no return gift of equal value is expected, though a prompt thank-you call or note when one arrives is very much part of the custom. Gifts are not sent to a household in mourning. And while younger generations and many companies are quietly scaling the tradition down, the December oseibo floors of the big department stores remain some of the busiest retail theatre in Japan.

If a Japanese colleague or friend's family sends you one, you have been counted. Accept it warmly, say thank you clearly — and know that the box of juice was never really about the juice.
