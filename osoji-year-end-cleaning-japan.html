Osoji: Japan's Great Year-End Cleaning, Done to Welcome a God

In the last days of December, Japan does something no spring-cleaning country quite matches: it cleans everything, all at once, on a deadline set by a god. Windows, kitchen grease, the tops of cupboards, the office desks, the school classrooms — all of it is scrubbed before the year turns. This is osoji, the great cleaning, and it is not really about hygiene. A home is cleaned at the end of the year because someone important is coming to stay in it.

When

The traditional starting gun is December 13, still called shogatsu koto hajime — the day New Year preparations begin. The date comes from the old soot-sweeping day of Edo Castle, when the shogun's household swept a year's worth of soot from its ceilings, and the custom spread from there. Most modern homes squeeze the work into the days after the last workday of the year, and companies and schools do their own version before closing. The hard deadline is the same for everyone: finish before the New Year arrives.

What happens

The guest being prepared for is Toshigami, the god of the new year, who visits each home at the turn of the year bringing health and good fortune. You would not receive an honoured guest in a dirty house, so the house is put right — and in the process, the year's accumulated grime, clutter, and bad luck go out with the rubbish. The ancestor of the custom, susuharai, was literally soot-sweeping: in the age of open hearths, a year's soot coated every ceiling, and temples and shrines still hold their great soot-sweeping in December, monks on ladders with long bamboo brooms, dusting the statues and rafters.

The cleaning has a border on the other side, too. Sweeping is avoided in the first days of January — you might sweep the new year's luck straight out of the door — and many people are careful to be done before the 31st rather than clean into the holiday itself. The sequence is the meaning: clean, close the year, and then rest in a house that is ready.

Good to know

If you live in Japan, osoji will find you whether you plan it or not: employers schedule it, landlords hint at it, and the shops fill with ladders, dusters, and heavy-duty sprays from mid-December. Join in properly and it stops feeling like a chore with a deadline. You are not tidying. You are clearing the year out of the rooms, so the next one has somewhere clean to land.
