The Emperor's Birthday in Japan

Standing in the plaza inside the Imperial Palace grounds, in a crowd waving small paper flags, I had the odd thought that almost everyone around me was older than the holiday we were celebrating. The Emperor's birthday in Japan falls on February 23 — but only since 2020. Before that it was December 23, and before that April 29. It is the one national holiday that is, quite literally, a person's birthday, and every time the reign changes, the day off moves house.

When

The current Emperor, Naruhito, was born on February 23, 1960, and his birthday has been a national holiday since 2020, the first full year of his reign. The transition left a strange gap: in 2019 Japan had no Emperor's birthday holiday at all, because the previous Emperor abdicated in April of that year and the new birthday had not yet come round. Earlier birthdays have not vanished from the calendar — they have been renamed. The Meiji Emperor's November 3 is now Culture Day, and the Showa Emperor's April 29 is now Showa Day, a holiday in his memory. The Heisei Emperor's December 23, by contrast, simply stopped being a holiday when he stepped down — the only one of the four that left no trace.

What happens

The centre of the day is the ippan sanga, the general public greeting at the Imperial Palace in Tokyo. On the morning of February 23, the palace grounds — closed to the public almost all year — open their gates, and visitors pass through to the plaza before the Chowaden reception hall. The Emperor appears on the balcony, behind glass, with members of the Imperial Family beside him, and addresses the crowd; the crowd answers by waving its flags all at once, which from inside it feels less like applause than weather. He appears several times through the morning, and visitors also sign a greeting register at the palace. The same greeting happens once more each year, on January 2 for the New Year — these are the only two days the inner grounds open this way.

Away from the palace, the day is quiet. National flags appear at the entrances of homes and public buildings, as they do on other national holidays, though fewer houses fly them than once did. There is no food, no costume, no ritual meal attached to the date — most people simply take the day off, late in February, when the plum blossom is out and the cold is beginning to loosen.

Good to know

The greeting is popular and the security checks are real: arrive early, expect bag checks and a slow, well-organised shuffle through the gates, and know that entry for the morning appearances closes well before the last one ends. What stayed with me was not the ceremony itself — it lasts only a few minutes — but the register books, and the care with which ordinary visitors wrote their names and addresses to be kept at the palace. A birthday, in most countries, is private. In Japan, once a reign, the whole country is on the guest list, and the date of your own day off depends on when one man was born.
