Coming of Age Day in Japan: Seijin no Hi

On one cold January morning, the trains fill with twenty-year-olds dressed like it is the most important day of their lives — because for them, it is. The young women wear brilliant long-sleeved kimono, the young men wear sharp suits, and the whole country stops to say: you are adults now.

When

Coming of Age Day, Seijin no Hi, is a national holiday on the second Monday of January. (Until 2000 it was fixed on January 15.) Every city and town holds a ceremony for the residents who turned twenty during that school year, so the guests of honour are the people you went to school with — in the town where you grew up.

What happens

The ceremony itself, the seijinshiki, is run by the local government: speeches, a welcome from the mayor, sometimes a concert or a message from a famous former resident. But the real spectacle is the clothing. Young women wear furisode, the formal kimono with long swinging sleeves worn by unmarried women, in bright reds, pinks, and patterns, with their hair professionally arranged and decorated. Young men mostly wear suits, though some choose the traditional dark haori jacket and hakama trousers.

Afterwards comes the part everyone actually remembers: the reunion. Old classmates who have scattered to universities and jobs across the country are suddenly all back in their hometown at once. Photos are taken in enormous groups, and in the evening the celebrations move to restaurants and bars — for many, this is the first time they can legally drink together, since the drinking age in Japan is twenty.

The day works because it is two things at once: a formal line drawn by the adults — speeches, certificates, best behaviour — and a homecoming party organised by the young people themselves. You are welcomed into society in the morning, and by nightfall you are back with the friends you had when you were twelve.

Good to know

In April 2022, Japan lowered the legal age of adulthood from twenty to eighteen. Most towns, however, kept their ceremonies for twenty-year-olds — many simply renamed the event a "gathering of twenty-year-olds" — and because the drinking and smoking ages stayed at twenty, twenty still feels like the real line. A furisode is expensive, so many are rented, and salons take bookings for hair and dressing a year or more in advance.

One city is famous for doing it differently: Kitakyushu, in the south, is known across Japan for its flamboyant ceremonies, where some young men arrive in wildly decorated outfits, bright dyed hair, and banners — a proud local tradition that television crews return to every single year.
