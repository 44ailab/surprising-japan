Bonenkai: Japan's Year-End Parties

The first bonenkai I went to, I spent the evening doing a job I had not known existed: watching a senior colleague's glass. In Japan, you do not pour your own drink at a party like this — you pour for others, and others pour for you — and an empty glass in front of the boss is a small failure of the whole table. Only halfway through the night did I ask what the party was actually for, and the answer was in its name. Bonenkai means "forget-the-year gathering." We were not celebrating the year. We were holding a small, organised ceremony for leaving it behind.

When

Bonenkai season is December, from the first week to the last working day of the year. A single person may attend several — one for the department, one for the whole company, one for the project team, one for old university friends — and the calendar fills fast. So does the city's supply of party rooms: popular izakaya and restaurants take bonenkai bookings weeks or months ahead, and finding a private room for thirty people in mid-December is a small war fought by phone in October.

What happens

The standard shape is a course meal and all-you-can-drink at an izakaya or restaurant, opened with a short speech and a toast, closed a couple of hours later with a formal round of thanks. Between the two, the year's accounts are settled socially: thanks are said out loud, apologies get made sideways, the new transfer meets the team properly for the first time, and things are said across the table that could not be said across a desk. Then, very often, the night moves: the nijikai, the second round, usually karaoke or a smaller bar, where the senior staff thin out and the conversation loosens again. A third round is not unknown.

None of it happens by itself. Every bonenkai has a kanji, the organiser — usually a younger member of the team — who books the venue, negotiates the course, collects the money from everyone, herds the group between rounds, and makes sure the boss gets home. It is real work, done invisibly, and it falls to the same person who can least refuse it. A good kanji is remembered; the role is an unofficial first test of running something.

The ceremony is changing, though. The pressure to drink — once close to compulsory — has eased hard in recent years: soft drinks at the toast are unremarkable now, non-drinkers are left alone, and some teams hold their bonenkai at lunch, or skip the second round, or let people simply go home. Younger staff are more willing to say the quiet part, that a compulsory party is a kind of work, and companies have begun, slowly, to listen.

Good to know

If you are invited to a bonenkai, the rules that matter are small ones: do not pour for yourself, hold your glass with both hands when a senior pours for you, and never let the evening end without thanking the kanji, who did more for your night than anyone. What surprised me most, over the years, was how seriously the forgetting is taken. The speeches are short, the grievances genuinely dropped, at least until January. A country that keeps so many ceremonies for remembering also built one — with a booking war, a second round, and a tired organiser — for letting a hard year go.
