Hanami: Cherry Blossom Viewing in Japan

For about one week each spring, the whole country plans its weekends around a flower. Hanami means "flower viewing," and it means spreading a sheet under the cherry trees in a park and eating, drinking, and talking there for hours.

When

The cherry blossoms open from late March to April in most regions. Television weather forecasts follow the "cherry blossom front" as it moves north across Japan, and the whole country makes its plans around it, because the flowers live for only about one week.

What happens

The hard work happens before the party: somebody must take a place under the trees. In companies, this job traditionally goes to the newest worker, who may arrive at a famous park early in the morning and sit on a blue plastic sheet all day to keep the place. Groups of friends take turns. In the evening, people also enjoy yozakura, cherry blossoms lit up in the dark.

The food is picnic food: lunch boxes, rice balls, fried chicken, and hanami dango — three soft dumplings on a stick, in pink, white, and green. The colors mean the blossoms, the snow of winter that has passed, and the green of spring that is coming. For about two weeks, shops sell everything in pink packages with a sakura flavor: drinks, sweets, even potato chips.

When the sun goes down, the day is not necessarily over. Lit up in the dark, the same trees look completely different from the daytime party beneath them. A famous park can hold both crowds in one day: the lunch groups on their sheets in the afternoon, and quiet walkers under the lights in the evening.

All of this rests on timing. The blossoms open, the forecasts move north, the place-keeper is sent out, and for a few days the park belongs to the flowers. Then the petals fall, the sheets are folded away, and the park goes back to normal — which is exactly why nobody wants to miss the week.

Good to know

Never step on the sheet with your shoes on. Everyone takes their shoes off at the edge of the sheet and lines them up neatly outside, just like at the door of a house.

Do not shake the branches, and do not pick the flowers. Take photographs and eat under the trees, but leave the blossoms on the tree. The point of hanami is to be with the flowers while they last — everyone knows the same park will look completely ordinary again in a week.
