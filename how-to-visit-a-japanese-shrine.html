SURPRISING JAPAN

How to Visit a Japanese Shrine

The first time I visited a shrine in Japan, I walked straight down the middle of the approach path, rinsed my hands in the wrong order, and then — proud of myself — clapped in front of the hall exactly the way I had seen everyone else do, except I was at a Buddhist temple, where nobody claps. Nobody had ever told me how to visit a Japanese shrine, so I copied strangers and got the details wrong. The real sequence takes two minutes to learn: a bow at the gate, water over the hands, a coin, two bows, two claps, one bow. Here it is, step by step.

When

You can visit a shrine on any day of the year, and most grounds are open from early morning. The busiest season by far is the first days of January, when millions of people make hatsumode, the first shrine visit of the New Year. Shrines also fill up during their own festivals, and in November, when families bring children of seven, five, and three for Shichi-Go-San. Go on an ordinary weekday morning and you may have the grounds almost to yourself — the steps below are exactly the same either way.

What happens

At the gate. A shrine begins at its torii gate. Bow once, lightly, before you pass through. Then walk up the approach path, the sando, keeping to one side: the centre of the path is reserved for the shrine's deity, the kami, and walking straight down the middle is considered bad manners.

Wash your hands. Near the entrance stands the temizu basin, a stone trough of water with long-handled ladles. Take a ladle in your right hand and pour water over your left hand, then swap and wash your right. Next, pour a little water into your cupped left hand and rinse your mouth — never drink directly from the ladle, and never put the ladle to your lips. Finally, tilt the ladle upright so the remaining water runs down and rinses the handle for the next person, and put it back.

At the main hall. Stand before the offering box. If there is a bell rope, give it a shake to ring the bell. Toss a coin into the box — a five-yen coin is the classic choice, because "five yen", go-en, sounds like the word for a good connection or lucky bond. Then: bow deeply twice, clap your hands twice, keep your hands together while you say your wish or thanks silently, and finish with one more deep bow. Two bows, two claps, one bow — ni-rei, ni-hakushu, ichi-rei.

Afterwards. Many visitors draw an omikuji, a paper fortune sold for a small fee. If the fortune is good, take it home. If it is bad, the custom is to tie it to the rack or pine branch provided on the grounds and leave the bad luck behind. What surprised me most, the first time I watched this, was how cheerfully people do it — a terrible fortune gets tied up like a decoration, and its owner walks off smiling. You will also see walls of small wooden plaques called ema, each carrying someone's handwritten wish — for exam success, a safe birth, a recovery. Writing one costs a few hundred yen, and nobody minds if your wish is in English.

Good to know

The clapping is the giveaway that you are at a Shinto shrine. At a Buddhist temple, the same moment is silent: no claps, just palms pressed together. Mixing the two up is the most common visitor mistake, and the easiest to avoid. A few more quiet rules: do not eat, drink, or smoke while strolling the grounds; ask before photographing people at prayer, and never photograph inside the main hall; and if you collect goshuin — the handsome calligraphy stamps shrines and temples issue — hand your stamp book to the office rather than expecting one at the offering box. None of this is policed. It is simply how the place is shared.
