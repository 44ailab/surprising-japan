Children's Day in Japan: Koinobori and Kabuto

In late April, fish appear in the sky. Long, hollow cloth carp called koinobori fly from poles at houses with children, moving in the wind like real fish swimming through the air. They are the sign of Children's Day.

When

Children's Day is May 5, part of the Golden Week holidays. The carp go up in April and May, and in late April many apartment balconies quietly fill with small koinobori sets made for city homes.

What happens

The carp is a symbol of strength. In an old Chinese story, a carp that swims up a waterfall becomes a dragon — and parents wish the same strength for their children. The carp are a family: the biggest carp at the top is black, and it is the father. The red carp under it is the mother, and the smaller blue and green carp below are the children. In the past, families added one carp for each son; today, many families add one for each child.

Inside houses with boys, families may display a kabuto, a samurai helmet, or a small set of armor, as symbols of protection and courage. In the evening, many families put iris leaves in the bath. This bath is called shobu-yu, and people say it protects the family from bad things.

Put together, a Children's Day home has three signs. Outside, the carp fly above the roof or the balcony. Inside, the helmet stands on display. And on the table there is a wrapped rice cake whose leaf carries a wish. None of these customs is large on its own. Together they say one thing in three different ways: grow up strong, be protected, and let the family continue. That is why the same three signs appear, quietly, in town after town every spring.

Good to know

The food of the day is different in different regions. In most of eastern Japan, people eat kashiwamochi: a rice cake with sweet bean paste inside, wrapped in an oak leaf. In much of western Japan, people eat chimaki: sweet rice dumplings wrapped in bamboo leaves.

The oak leaf is a symbol of a family that continues, because an oak tree does not drop its old leaves until the new leaves grow. Parents eat the cake and wish that their family line will continue in the same way. But you do not eat the leaf — it is only a wrapping.
