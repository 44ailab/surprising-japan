Higan: Japan's Equinox Weeks When Families Visit Graves

Twice a year, the trains fill with people carrying flowers, buckets, and sweet rice cakes, all heading for the family grave. There is no festival, no parade, no special food stall — just a quiet week when Japan goes to visit its ancestors, in spring and again in autumn.

When

Higan lasts seven days, built around the equinox: the three days before the spring equinox (around March 20) and the three days after it, and the same again around the autumn equinox (around September 22 or 23). Both equinox days themselves are national holidays. The word higan means "the other shore" in Buddhism — the shore of enlightenment — as opposed to shigan, "this shore," the world we live in now.

What happens

The heart of Higan is ohaka-mairi, visiting the family grave. Families pull weeds, scrub the gravestone with water, replace the flowers, light incense, and leave offerings of food and drink their ancestors liked. Temples hold special Higan services, and because the equinox days are holidays, whole families can travel back to their hometowns together.

At home there is one traditional sweet: a soft rice cake coated in sweet red bean paste. In spring it is called botamochi, after the botan (peony) that blooms then. In autumn the very same cake is called ohagi, after hagi (bush clover). Same food, two names — you are expected to know which one to say.

Why the equinox? At the equinox the sun rises exactly in the east and sets exactly in the west, and in Pure Land Buddhism paradise lies in the west. Tradition says the other shore feels closest at Higan — and a well-known saying adds a practical note: atsusa samusa mo higan made, "the heat and the cold last only until Higan." After the equinox, the season really turns.

Good to know

Higan is one of the busiest times of year at cemeteries, and flower shops near graveyards do a roaring trade. The exact dates of the equinox holidays shift slightly from year to year, because they are set by the actual astronomical equinox and announced officially the year before. If you visit a Japanese friend's family grave at Higan, bring flowers and put your hands together — no one will expect more than that.
