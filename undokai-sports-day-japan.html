Undokai: Sports Day in Japan

On a Saturday in autumn, almost every elementary school in Japan becomes a festival ground. Undokai, sports day, is not only a competition — it is a community festival that the children have practiced for many weeks.

When

Undokai is held on a Saturday in autumn. The preparation starts long before: children rehearse group dances and races for weeks, and the teacher chooses the last runner of the relay — the anchor — well in advance. It is a special honor, and children remember for years if they were chosen.

What happens

The whole school is divided into two teams, red and white. The children wear caps that can be turned inside out — red on one side, white on the other. There are group dances, relay races, tug of war, and the famous tamaire: small children throw many small balls into a basket on top of a high pole, before the time runs out.

Parents and grandparents watch from the edges of the school ground. Near the finish line of the races there is a small forest of cameras and tripods, and parents film everything, even when their child is only a small dot far away. At lunchtime, families sit on sheets and eat a picnic lunch together: lunch boxes made at home that morning, rice balls, fried chicken, and rolled omelet.

Everything is a team matter. Your cap says which side you are on, the relay ends with the anchor everyone watches, and even tamaire is a race against the clock that the whole team counts together. A child who is not fast can still be the hero of the ball toss, and a child who is fast carries the hopes of half the school in the last meters of the relay. That balance — many ways to matter, all of them public — is what makes the day feel so big to the children in it.

Good to know

In many schools, parents take a good place to sit early in the morning, putting down a sheet, sometimes with a name card on it. But more and more schools now limit or ban this early place-taking, because it causes arguments. Always follow your school's rules.

The teachers organize everything, judge the races, and dance with the youngest children. At the end of the day, the parents clap for the teachers, like actors at the end of a play — a small moment that tells you undokai belongs to the whole community, not just the children.
