Nengajo: Japan's New Year Cards, Delivered All on One Morning

On the morning of January 1, the mailbox of a Japanese home does something it never does on any other day: it fills up, all at once, with a stack of postcards as thick as a paperback. Every one of them was written in December. Every one was held back by the post office. And every one arrives on the same morning, by design. These are nengajo, the New Year greeting cards of Japan — less a card than a national appointment, kept by hundreds of millions of pieces of paper at once.

When

The cards go on sale at the start of November, and the season runs on rails. In most years the post office starts accepting nengajo in mid-December and asks you to post by December 25 to be sure of January 1 delivery. The dates move a little from year to year — for the 2027 New Year, acceptance starts on December 20 and the requested posting date is December 27 — so check the current year's announcement before you rely on the old rhythm. Cards posted too early are treated as ordinary mail and arrive before the year turns, which spoils the whole effect.

What happens

Through December, people write to family, friends, teachers, colleagues, and bosses: a zodiac animal for the coming year, a printed photograph of the children, a few lines of thanks for the year gone by. The post office collects the flood, sorts it, and holds it. Then, on the morning of January 1, delivery staff across the country set out at once, and the cards land in bundles. The official postcards carry lottery numbers along the bottom, drawn in January — a greeting that might also win you a prize.

The system has rules with teeth. You do not send a nengajo to a household in mourning; they will have sent you a plain notice in November or December asking you to hold your greetings this year, and you honour it. And if a card arrives from someone you did not send one to, you reply — a return card posted in the first week of January is still a New Year card, and after that it becomes a winter greeting instead.

Good to know

The flood is shrinking. At its peak in 2003, Japan Post produced more than four billion New Year postcards; group messages and social media take a larger share every year. But the bundle in the mailbox has not disappeared, and in business it still matters: a missing card is noticed in a way a missing message never is. If you live in Japan, the practical move is simple — buy the cards in November, write them before the Christmas rush, and post them inside the window. The post office does the rest, in one morning, all at once.
