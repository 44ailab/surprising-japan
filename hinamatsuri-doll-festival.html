Hinamatsuri: Japan's Doll Festival

On Hinamatsuri, the living room turns into a tiny imperial court. Rows of dolls in thousand-year-old court clothing sit on red steps, and the whole display exists for one reason: to wish for the health and happiness of the family's daughters.

When

Hinamatsuri is on March 3. The doll display usually comes out in the middle of February, so the dolls watch over the house for about two weeks before the day itself. Grandparents often buy the doll set when a girl is born.

What happens

The Emperor and Empress sit on the top step. Below them are ladies of the court, musicians, and small furniture, all in the clothes of the imperial court of about a thousand years ago. A full set can have seven steps. In small modern apartments, many families choose a small glass case with only the Emperor and Empress.

On the day itself, families eat chirashizushi — sushi rice in a bowl with colorful seafood and vegetables scattered on top. They also serve hina-arare, small sweet rice crackers in pink, white, and green, and hishimochi, a diamond-shaped rice cake with three layers in the same colors. The colors mean spring flowers, snow, and new green plants. Adults may drink shirozake, a sweet white sake, while children drink a non-alcoholic sweet drink called amazake.

For about two weeks, the doll display is the center of the home. It is not hidden in a child's room — it stands where everyone can see it, a small court in formal dress, watching over the family until the day itself arrives. Then, almost as quickly as it appeared, it is gone again for another year.

Good to know

There is a famous superstition about putting the dolls away. If a family is slow to pack them away after March 3, people say the daughter's marriage will also be late. Many families put everything back in the box on March 4, and some turn the dolls to face the wall on the night of March 3, as a promise to pack them soon.

The left-right position of the Emperor and Empress changes by region. In the Tokyo style, the Emperor sits on the left when you look at the display; in the older Kyoto style, he sits on the right. Both are correct. And the dolls are not toys — children are told to look, but not touch. A full set is expensive, and families keep it for decades.
