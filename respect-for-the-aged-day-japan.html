Respect for the Aged Day in Japan: Keiro no Hi

Japan has the longest-lived people on earth, and one Monday every September is set aside entirely for them. Grandparents receive drawings and phone calls, towns hold ceremonies for their oldest residents, and the news reports — with real pride — just how many people in the country are over one hundred years old.

When

Respect for the Aged Day, Keiro no Hi, is a national holiday on the third Monday of September. It began much smaller: in 1947, one village in Hyogo prefecture set aside a day to respect its elders and celebrate long life. The idea spread across the country, and in 1966 it became a national holiday, fixed on September 15. In 2003 it moved to the third Monday, so it now forms a long weekend.

What happens

Towns and neighbourhoods hold ceremonies honouring their elderly residents, and volunteers deliver special boxed lunches to older people who live alone. Families visit or call grandparents, and small children make drawings and crafts as gifts. For many families it is the autumn counterpart of a birthday: a fixed day when you are expected to say thank you to the oldest generation.

The government marks the day too. Every Japanese citizen who turns one hundred receives a commemorative silver cup and a letter of congratulation — a custom that began in 1963. Back then there were only about 150 centenarians in the whole country. There are now over 90,000, and the numbers grew so large that in 2016 the cups were quietly changed from solid silver to silver-plated. Around this holiday each year, the health ministry publishes the new count, and it makes the national news every time.

Some years, the calendar lines up kindly: Respect for the Aged Day on Monday, the autumn equinox a few days later, and a one-off "citizen's holiday" sandwiched between them can combine into a five-day September break. The Japanese call it Silver Week — the autumn answer to Golden Week.

Good to know

Long life is genuinely celebrated in Japan, not just politely acknowledged — turning one hundred is treated as an achievement the whole community shares. If you have elderly Japanese neighbours or relatives, a small gift or even a card on this day will be noticed and remembered. Sweets and flowers are safe choices; the gesture matters far more than the size.
