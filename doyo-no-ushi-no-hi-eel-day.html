Doyo no Ushi no Hi: Japan's Midsummer Eel Day

Once a summer, on a day decided by an old calendar and the twelve animal signs, Japan sits down to eat eel. Grilled, glazed, and served over rice, unagi on this day is not just lunch — it is the country's traditional defence against the exhausting heat.

When

On the old Japanese calendar, doyo is the period of about eighteen days before each season changes. The summer doyo, ending just before the start of autumn in early August, is the famous one. Inside it falls a Day of the Ox — Ushi no Hi, from the twelve-animal cycle of days. That day, usually in late July, is Doyo no Ushi no Hi. Some years have two Ox days in the summer doyo, and then eel gets two big days: the first and the second.

What happens

The dish is unagi no kabayaki: eel filleted, grilled, steamed, and grilled again with a sweet soy glaze, served over a bed of rice. On the day itself, eel restaurants have queues out the door, and supermarkets build great displays of eel that have been stockpiled for weeks. Families who rarely buy eel — it is a luxury — buy it on this day, because the custom says its richness protects you from natsubate, the drained, appetite-less fatigue of the Japanese summer.

The idea is older than the eel. An old belief held that foods beginning with the sound "u" help the body through the doyo days — eel (unagi), noodles (udon), pickled plums (umeboshi), gourds (uri). Eel, the richest of them, simply won.

So the day runs on a piece of wordplay that became a national habit: the calendar says Ox, the old saying says "u", and dinner is decided. In the worst heat of the year, Japan eats its most fortifying food on schedule, together.

Good to know

The story usually told about how eel won involves a struggling eel shop in the Edo period and the scholar and inventor Hiraga Gennai. Asked how to sell eel in summer — then its off-season — Gennai is said to have suggested a shop sign playing on the "u" foods belief: eat eel on the Day of the Ox and the summer will not defeat you. The sign worked rather too well; two centuries later, the whole country still obeys it.

One modern note: wild Japanese eel has become scarce and expensive, so for many families the midsummer eel is now a modest portion, carefully chosen — which somehow makes the ritual feel more, not less, like a treat.
