Otsukimi: Japan's Moon-Viewing Festival

The autumn moon is said to be the most beautiful moon of the year, and Japan has a festival for simply looking at it. No games, no noise — just dumplings, tall grass, and a rabbit in the moon making rice cakes.

When

The main night is Jugoya, "the fifteenth night," the night of the harvest moon in autumn. About one month later comes Jusanya, "the thirteenth night," a second moon viewing for a moon that is not quite full.

What happens

The preparation is simple and beautiful. On a small stand near a window or on the veranda, the family puts a pyramid of round white dumplings called tsukimi dango, a vase of tall pampas grass called susuki, and some autumn foods like taro and chestnuts. Then the family simply looks at the moon.

In Japanese stories, there is no man in the moon. There is a rabbit, and the rabbit is making rice cakes with a big wooden hammer and a mortar. For Jugoya, the pyramid is often made of fifteen dumplings — one for the fifteenth night — and after the moon viewing, the family eats them. People say this brings health and happiness.

The two nights belong together. Jugoya is the full, famous one — the harvest moon at its roundest. Jusanya, about a month later, is quieter: a moon that is not quite full, watched by the families who know the rule about never viewing only one. Together they stretch the moon season across the whole of autumn, from the first cool evenings to the edge of winter.

And the festival asks almost nothing of you. There is no ticket, no crowd, and no performance. You make the dumplings, you stand the grass in a vase, and you look up. In a year full of busy festivals, otsukimi is the one that celebrates doing less, together, under the same moon.

Good to know

Looking at only one of the two moons is said to bring bad luck. It is called katamizuki, "looking at only one side." If your family celebrates Jugoya, you should also celebrate Jusanya.

Susuki grass is more than a decoration. Its leaves are sharp, and people believed they could cut evil, and the tall grass also stands for a good rice harvest. The moon follows you home, too: in September, even fast-food shops sell special "tsukimi" burgers with a round fried egg as the moon, and the moon rabbit appears in advertisements everywhere.
