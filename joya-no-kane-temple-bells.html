Joya no Kane: Why Temple Bells Ring 108 Times on New Year's Eve

There is no countdown in the Japanese New Year, no ball drop, no shouting in the street. Instead, in the cold last hour of the year, a great bronze bell begins to sound from the temples — slowly, one strike at a time, each note allowed to fade completely before the next — 108 times in all, the last strike falling after midnight. This is joya no kane, the bell of New Year's Eve, and it is the sound the old year leaves by.

When

The ringing happens on the night of December 31, Omisoka. At many temples it is arranged across the border of the years: 107 strikes in the old year, and the final, 108th strike after midnight, so the sound itself carries you from one year into the next. Crowds gather in temple courtyards in the dark, breath steaming, listening.

What happens

The number is the teaching. In Buddhism, human beings are troubled by 108 earthly desires and attachments — bonno — the wants and angers and delusions that cloud a life. Each strike of the bell is said to drive one of them away, so that a person can enter the new year with a cleared heart. One traditional count builds the number from the senses, the feelings, and the times of a life, multiplied together; what matters on the night is simpler. You stand in the cold, you listen, and 108 times the bell tells you to let something go.

Who strikes the bell depends on the temple. At the great temples the monks do it themselves — at Chion-in in Kyoto, a team of seventeen monks swings the heavy beam against the enormous bell in a rehearsed, athletic ceremony that draws crowds of watchers. At smaller neighbourhood temples, the visitors ring it: you queue in the dark, take the rope or the beam for your one strike, make a silent wish, and step aside for the next person.

Good to know

You do not need a ticket or a belief to take part — a local temple on New Year's Eve is one of the most open doors in Japanese religious life, and many hand out hot sweet sake to the people waiting. Go to a small temple if you want to strike the bell yourself, and to a famous one if you want to hear a great bell struck properly. Either way, dress for standing still outdoors at midnight. The bell is slow, and that is the point.
