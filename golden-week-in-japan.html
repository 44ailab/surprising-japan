Golden Week in Japan: Why the Whole Country Takes a Week Off

Golden Week is the moment Japan exhales. In late April and early May, four national holidays land almost back to back, offices empty out, and it feels like the entire country is on a train going somewhere else.

When

Golden Week is built from four holidays: Showa Day on April 29, Constitution Memorial Day on May 3, Greenery Day on May 4, and Children's Day on May 5. Add the weekends at both ends — and a rule that moves a holiday to Monday when it falls on a Sunday — and many people end up with a week or more off work. It comes just after April, when the school year and the business year both begin in Japan, so it is the first long rest of the new year.

What happens

People travel. Families go back to their hometowns, couples and friends take domestic trips, and airports fill with people heading overseas. Famous sightseeing spots see their busiest days of the year, express trains are packed, highways jam for hours, and hotel prices climb to their peak. Many offices and factories simply close for the whole stretch rather than open on the single working days in between.

The last of the holidays, Children's Day on May 5, is the gentle finale: carp streamers fly over houses and rivers, and families wish for their children to grow up strong. Then, almost overnight, the country goes back to work.

For visitors, Golden Week is a strange double experience. The cities feel festive and alive, but this is the one week when Japan is travelling to see itself — so you will be sightseeing together with the whole population.

Good to know

The name is not an old tradition at all. It was coined in 1951 by a film company executive who noticed that cinema attendance jumped during these holidays, and borrowed the radio term "golden time" for it. A week of holidays invented by the calendar got its name from the movie business.

There is also a word for what comes after: gogatsubyo, "May sickness" — the slump people feel when the long holiday ends and work or school starts again. Every May, the country quietly agrees that getting back to normal is the hardest part of the holiday.
