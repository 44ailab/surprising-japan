Tanabata: Japan's Star Festival

Tanabata is a love story written in the sky, and a festival where the main event is handwriting. All over Japan, people write their wishes on thin strips of colored paper and hang them on bamboo, hoping the stars are listening.

When

Tanabata night is July 7 in most regions. In the old story, Orihime, a weaver princess, and Hikoboshi, a cowherd, fell in love and stopped working. As a punishment, they were separated by the Milky Way, and they can meet only once a year, on Tanabata night — and only if the sky is clear.

What happens

For children, Tanabata has one main job: write a wish. The wish goes on a tanzaku, a long, thin strip of colored paper. People hang the tanzaku on the branches of bamboo, together with paper decorations: chains, nets, cranes, and long streamers. Schools, train stations, and shopping streets put up bamboo in early July, and anyone can add a wish.

For dinner, many families eat somen — very thin white noodles, served cold with a dipping sauce. It is easy food for a hot, humid night. People say the white noodles look like the Milky Way, or like the threads of Orihime's weaving.

The bamboo is an open invitation. You do not need to be a child, or a student, or anyone in particular: if there is a blank strip of paper and a pen, your wish belongs on the branch next to everyone else's. For one week, train stations and shopping streets fill with the handwriting of strangers — wishes for health, for exams, for love, for the two stars to meet under a clear sky.

Good to know

Tanzaku have five traditional colors: blue or green, red, yellow, white, and black or purple. They come from an old philosophy of five elements, and each color has a meaning — growth, thanks to family ancestors, honesty, duty, and study. Most children simply choose the color they like, but the paper sets are sold in these five colors.

After the festival, shrines often burn the bamboo and the wishes, so the wishes go up to the sky with the smoke. In the past, people also floated the bamboo down rivers, sending the wishes away on the water instead.

And the date moves: in some regions, people kept the old lunar calendar, and Tanabata comes in August. The famous Sendai Tanabata Festival, with its giant colorful streamers, is held in August. So the star lovers get two seasons of wishes — July in most of Japan, and August where the old calendar survived — and the bamboo branches go up twice in one summer.
