Marine Day and Mountain Day: Japan's Holidays for the Sea and the Peaks

Japan is an island country covered in mountains, and it has done something no other country has: it gave both of them a national holiday. In July the country thanks the sea. In August it thanks the mountains. Offices close for both.

When

Marine Day, Umi no Hi, falls on the third Monday of July. Mountain Day, Yama no Hi, falls on August 11. They are a pair in spirit but arrived twenty years apart: Marine Day was first celebrated in 1996, while Mountain Day only began in 2016 — making it Japan's newest national holiday.

What happens

Marine Day commemorates a voyage: in 1876 the Emperor Meiji toured northern Japan aboard the Meiji-maru and returned safely to Yokohama port on July 20. The holiday was originally fixed on that date, and moved to the third Monday of July in 2003. Its official purpose is to give thanks for the blessings of the sea and to wish for the prosperity of Japan as a maritime nation. In practice it marks the height of summer — beaches hold their official opening season, ports and aquariums run special events, and anyone who can heads for the water.

Mountain Day has no single historical event behind it. It was created simply as a chance, in the words of the law, "to become familiar with the mountains and appreciate their benefits" — in a country where about seventy percent of the land is mountainous, and where mountains supply the water, the timber, and much of the scenery. Supporters explained that August 11 was chosen because the character for eight, 八, widens at the bottom like a mountain slope, and the two ones in 11 look like a pair of standing trees.

Together the two days show something true about the Japanese calendar: it does not only honour emperors, constitutions, and ancestors. It also stops, twice a summer, to say thank you to the landscape itself.

Good to know

Mountain Day filled a gap. With its arrival, every month of the Japanese year has at least one national holiday — except June, which still has none and is famous for it. Japan has sixteen national holidays in total, among the highest counts in the world. If you are planning a trip, note that both Marine Day and the mid-August Obon season make trains and mountain lodges extremely busy; book early or travel against the flow.
