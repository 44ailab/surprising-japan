Nagoshi no Harae: Japan's Summer Purification Ritual

Halfway through the year, Japanese shrines offer a reset. A very large ring made of grass, taller than a person, stands in the shrine grounds — and if you walk through it the right way, you leave the mistakes and bad luck of the first six months behind.

When

Nagoshi no Harae is held on June 30, in the middle of tsuyu, the rainy season. June is not a festive month: the sky is gray for weeks, umbrellas appear everywhere, and hydrangeas turn blue and purple at temples. The purification fits the mood — a quiet washing-clean before the second half of the year.

Daily life changes in the rainy season. Laundry dries inside the house, convenience stores put umbrellas near the door, and the gray weeks stretch on. It is not a festive month, and that is the point: the year pauses, gets wet and quiet, and then, on its last day of June, offers one green ring to walk through.

What happens

The ring is called a chinowa. You walk through it in a special pattern, and people say this washes away the mistakes and bad luck of the first half of the year, so you can start the second half clean.

The route is precise. First, bow to the ring. Step through it with your left foot, walk in a circle to the left, and come back to the front. Go through again, this time starting with your right foot, and circle to the right. Then go through a third time, starting with the left foot again, and circle to the left. Your path makes the shape of the number 8. After that, you walk to the shrine and pray.

Good to know

In Kyoto, June has a special sweet called minazuki. It is a white jelly cake cut in a triangle, with red beans on top, and people eat it on June 30. The white triangle looks like ice — a wish for coolness in the hot summer — and the red beans are said to push away evil.

The rainy season has one flower people travel to see: ajisai, hydrangeas, which change color with the soil. Temple gardeners take great pride in them. Between the gray rain, the blue flowers, and one evening of walking through grass in a figure of eight, June quietly becomes one of the most atmospheric months of the Japanese year.
