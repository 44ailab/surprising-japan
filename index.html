SURPRISING JAPAN

Surprising Japan — A Year of Japanese Festivals, Customs, and Unwritten Rules

In Japan, you can feel the calendar. Every month, something changes. A decoration appears at the front door. A special food appears in the supermarket. Children start to practice for an event at school. Most of this is not for tourists. It is the normal rhythm of Japanese homes, repeated every year, and usually nobody explains it.

This book follows one year in a Japanese home, month by month. It is not a list of famous festivals. It explains the small, practical details that Japanese people do without thinking: the days when you should not put up a decoration, the correct way to walk through a big ring of grass at a shrine, why a sushi roll must be eaten in silence, and why fried chicken must be reserved many weeks before Christmas.

If you plan to visit Japan, if you live here, if your partner or family is Japanese, or if you are simply curious about daily life in this country, this book shows you the year as people really live it. We start in January, when the Japanese year truly begins.

Below is one short page for every chapter — a taste of each month. The full book goes deeper into every custom, every food, and every unwritten rule.

January Japanese New Year Traditions: Hatsumode, Osechi, and Otoshidama Door decorations, lucky foods in stacked boxes, greeting cards that all arrive on one morning, and money in small envelopes. February Setsubun: Japan's Bean-Throwing Festival Throwing beans at a demon, eating one for every year of your age, and a sushi roll you must finish in silence. February–March Valentine's Day and White Day in Japan: Chocolate in February, Payback in March Women give the chocolate in February — even to coworkers — and men pay it back, three times over, in March. March Hinamatsuri: Japan's Doll Festival An imperial court in miniature, pink-white-and-green sweets, and the superstition about packing the dolls away late. April Hanami: Cherry Blossom Viewing in Japan Blue sheets under the trees, the junior worker who guards the spot all day, and blossoms that last about a week. May Children's Day in Japan: Koinobori and Kabuto Cloth carp swimming in the sky, a samurai helmet indoors, and rice cakes wrapped in an oak leaf you do not eat. April–May Golden Week in Japan: Why the Whole Country Takes a Week Off Four holidays in one week, trains booked solid, and the gloom that arrives when it ends. June Nagoshi no Harae: Japan's Summer Purification Ritual Walking through a giant ring of grass in a figure of eight to wash away the first half of the year. July Tanabata: Japan's Star Festival Two separated stars, wishes written on colored paper strips, and bamboo branches full of hopes. July–August Japanese Fireworks Festivals: Hanabi, the Sound of Summer Shells a metre wide, crowds in yukata, and the old shout of "Tamaya!" over the river. August Obon: Japan's Festival of the Dead Welcome fires, a cucumber horse and an eggplant cow, and dancing in a circle anyone can join. September Otsukimi: Japan's Moon-Viewing Festival A pyramid of white dumplings, pampas grass, and the rabbit who makes rice cakes in the moon. October Undokai: Sports Day in Japan Red team against white team, balls thrown into a high basket, and the honor of running last in the relay. October–November Autumn Leaves in Japan: Koyo, the Country's Second Flower Season A forecast front for red leaves, night illuminations in temple gardens, and maple leaves fried as tempura. November Shichi-Go-San: Japan's Festival for Children Aged 7, 5, and 3 First full kimono, thousand-year candy in a crane-and-turtle bag, and photographs booked weeks ahead. December Christmas and New Year's Eve in Japan Fried chicken reserved weeks ahead, the great year-end cleaning, long noodles, and 108 temple bells.
