SURPRISING JAPAN

Surprising Japan — A Year of Japanese Festivals, Customs, and Unwritten Rules

In Japan, you can feel the calendar. Every month, something changes. A decoration appears at the front door. A special food appears in the supermarket. Children start to practice for an event at school. Most of this is not for tourists. It is the normal rhythm of Japanese homes, repeated every year, and usually nobody explains it.

This book follows one year in a Japanese home, month by month. It is not a list of famous festivals. It explains the small, practical details that Japanese people do without thinking: the days when you should not put up a decoration, the correct way to walk through a big ring of grass at a shrine, why a sushi roll must be eaten in silence, and why fried chicken must be reserved many weeks before Christmas.

If you plan to visit Japan, if you live here, if your partner or family is Japanese, or if you are simply curious about daily life in this country, this book shows you the year as people really live it. We start in January, when the Japanese year truly begins.

Below is one short page for every chapter — a taste of each month. The full book goes deeper into every custom, every food, and every unwritten rule.

January Japanese New Year Traditions: Hatsumode, Osechi, and Otoshidama Door decorations, lucky foods in stacked boxes, greeting cards that all arrive on one morning, and money in small envelopes. January Osechi: Japan's New Year Food Boxes Stacked boxes where every dish is a wish: black beans for health, golden mash for wealth — and nothing warm, by design. January Fukubukuro: Japan's Lucky Bags Sealed bags of mystery bargains, pre-dawn queues, and the gamble every January shopper takes. January Coming of Age Day in Japan: Seijin no Hi Twenty-year-olds in furisode and suits, a ceremony at city hall, and reunions that run all night. All Year How to Visit a Japanese Shrine Bow at the gate, wash your hands in the right order, two bows, two claps, one bow — and what to do with a bad fortune. All Year Seasonal Food in Japan: What to Eat in Every Season The menu belongs to the calendar: bamboo shoots in spring, eel in summer, matsutake in autumn, hot pot in winter. All Year Omiyage: Japan's Souvenir Gift Culture The box of wrapped sweets you owe the office after a trip — and why bringing back nothing is the one real mistake. February Setsubun: Japan's Bean-Throwing Festival Throwing beans at a demon, eating one for every year of your age, and a sushi roll you must finish in silence. February–March Valentine's Day and White Day in Japan: Chocolate in February, Payback in March Women give the chocolate in February — even to coworkers — and men pay it back, three times over, in March. February The Emperor's Birthday in Japan The one holiday whose date moves with the reign — and the day the Imperial Palace opens its gates to everyone. March Hinamatsuri: Japan's Doll Festival An imperial court in miniature, pink-white-and-green sweets, and the superstition about packing the dolls away late. March & September Higan: Japan's Equinox Weeks When Families Visit Graves Twice a year, families clean the graves, leave the same rice cake under two names, and wait out the turning of the season. April Hanami: Cherry Blossom Viewing in Japan Blue sheets under the trees, the junior worker who guards the spot all day, and blossoms that last about a week. May Children's Day in Japan: Koinobori and Kabuto Cloth carp swimming in the sky, a samurai helmet indoors, and rice cakes wrapped in an oak leaf you do not eat. April–May Golden Week in Japan: Why the Whole Country Takes a Week Off Four holidays in one week, trains booked solid, and the gloom that arrives when it ends. June Tsuyu: Japan's Rainy Season and the Hydrangea Weeks A rain front that climbs the country, paper dolls hung for sunshine, and hydrangeas that change colour with the soil. June Nagoshi no Harae: Japan's Summer Purification Ritual Walking through a giant ring of grass in a figure of eight to wash away the first half of the year. July Tanabata: Japan's Star Festival Two separated stars, wishes written on colored paper strips, and bamboo branches full of hopes. July Doyo no Ushi no Hi: Japan's Midsummer Eel Day Grilled eel on the hottest days, an old copywriter's slogan, and foods that start with "u". July–August Japanese Fireworks Festivals: Hanabi, the Sound of Summer Shells a metre wide, crowds in yukata, and the old shout of "Tamaya!" over the river. July & August Marine Day and Mountain Day: Japan's Holidays for the Sea and the Peaks A holiday for the sea in July, a holiday for the mountains in August — and a June with none at all. August Obon: Japan's Festival of the Dead Welcome fires, a cucumber horse and an eggplant cow, and dancing in a circle anyone can join. September Otsukimi: Japan's Moon-Viewing Festival A pyramid of white dumplings, pampas grass, and the rabbit who makes rice cakes in the moon. September Respect for the Aged Day in Japan: Keiro no Hi A village idea that became a national holiday, a silver cup for turning a hundred, and a long weekend named Silver Week. October Undokai: Sports Day in Japan Red team against white team, balls thrown into a high basket, and the honor of running last in the relay. October–November Autumn Leaves in Japan: Koyo, the Country's Second Flower Season A forecast front for red leaves, night illuminations in temple gardens, and maple leaves fried as tempura. July & December Ochugen and Oseibo: Japan's Gift-Giving Seasons Twice a year the gift sets arrive — thanks, not exchange, wrapped with a paper band that says why. November Culture Day in Japan: Bunka no Hi An emperor's birthday turned into a day for culture — free museums, school festivals, and famously clear skies. November Shichi-Go-San: Japan's Festival for Children Aged 7, 5, and 3 First full kimono, thousand-year candy in a crane-and-turtle bag, and photographs booked weeks ahead. November Labor Thanksgiving Day in Japan: Kinro Kansha no Hi An ancient harvest rite in modern dress: new rice offered in the palace, and thank-you drawings for the firefighters. December Christmas and New Year's Eve in Japan Fried chicken reserved weeks ahead, the great year-end cleaning, long noodles, and 108 temple bells. December Bonenkai: Japan's Year-End Parties Forget-the-year parties: December booking wars, second rounds, and the colleague quietly watching your glass.
